# Trust in Courts, Trust in Credit?

## 1. More trust in courts does not reliably predict more credit

**Exhibit**

One sentence beneath the question “Do firms tend to have more credit where managers trust the courts to be fair?”

> Across 163 economies, the association is small and uncertain.

**Speaker notes**

A lender may care whether it can recover money when a borrower defaults. The project checks whether that idea is visible in national survey figures on court perceptions and credit holding.

## 2. The comparison uses country data from the same survey wave

**Exhibit**

| What we measure | What it tells us |
|---|---|
| Court confidence score | How favorably managers view court impartiality in commercial disputes |
| Firms with a loan or credit line | How widespread credit holding is among eligible firms |

**Speaker notes**

Use the World Bank Enterprise Surveys national totals. Match the measures by economy and survey year. The confidence score reverses the published doubt index so higher means more confidence. The figures summarize national firm responses. The survey does not give outcomes for individual courts or individual loan applications.

## 3. Two country examples give opposite answers

**Exhibit**

| Lower-confidence country | Credit holding | Higher-confidence country | Credit holding |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Armenia (63.7) | 48.6% | Spain (86.4) | 61.7% |
| Brazil (67.7) | 57.6% | Trinidad and Tobago (75.5) | 28.6% |

Confidence scores are out of 100. Each pair shares a World Bank region and a survey year.

**Speaker notes**

The first pair fits the lending story; the second goes the other way. The appendix gives all four examples. The cases were drawn near the lower and upper confidence quartiles within eligible groups without using the credit figures. These examples show why we need an average comparison and an interval.

## 4. The average court-confidence estimate is small and uncertain

**Exhibit**

| Comparison | Credit difference per 10 more confidence points | 95% interval |
|---|---:|---:|
| All economies, confidence alone | +0.60 pp | [−1.39, 2.59] |
| Add bribery and firm size | −0.67 pp | [−2.41, 1.07] |
| Compare within the same region and survey year | −1.20 pp | [−3.09, 0.69] |

**Speaker notes**

The first comparison asks the question in its simplest terms. The adjusted model compares countries with similar observed bribery and average firm size. The last row compares countries within the same region and survey year, using 144 economies. All intervals include zero. We cannot confidently predict even the direction of the credit difference.

## 5. Comparing an economy with itself leaves the direction uncertain

**Exhibit**

> A 10-point fall in the share calling courts a serious obstacle is associated with **−0.61 pp** change in credit holding across 108 economies. The 95% interval is **[−3.92, 2.71]**.

**Speaker notes**

The archive has one wave per economy for the direct confidence question. The older question about severe court obstacles lets us compare two waves within the same economy. Allow for changes in bribery and firm size and for years between the waves. Other conditions and the mix of firms can change. The interval leaves room for either direction.

## 6. Bribery's association gets smaller when the comparison gets closer

**Exhibit**

| Model | Credit difference per 10 points more bribery | 95% interval |
|---|---:|---:|
| Basic adjusted model | −7.06 pp | [−9.38, −4.73] |
| Add region and survey cohort | −2.81 pp | [−5.53, −0.08] |
| Also add firms' website adoption | −1.43 pp | [−4.14, 1.28] |

**Speaker notes**

Bribery is the stronger correlate in the basic model. The same economies enter these comparisons. The gap shrinks as we account for other factors. Website use captures part of business development and can respond to credit or institutions. The final interval includes zero.

## 7. The legal family gives no precise guide to credit holding

**Exhibit**

| Reference group | English common-law credit difference | 95% interval |
|---|---:|---:|
| All documented other origins | −4.45 pp | [−11.34, 2.44] |
| Named civil-code families | −4.69 pp | [−11.80, 2.43] |

**Speaker notes**

Hold court confidence, bribery and firm size fixed. Legal origin describes the heritage of the commercial code. A code tells us little about how quickly a court enforces a judgment. Both intervals span gaps large enough to matter to a lender.

## 8. Diagnostics check how much weight to put on the model

**Exhibit**

| Check | What it showed |
|---|---|
| Breusch–Pagan | Little evidence of unequal residual variance in the core model |
| Durbin–Watson | Results change when the country order changes |
| VIF | The core predictors have little overlap in this model |

**Speaker notes**

We use HC3 standard errors to allow for uneven variance. Countries have no natural sequence, so we check shared geography and timing instead of interpreting neighboring residuals as a time series. Extra fit in the larger model can come from variables measured after borrowing or among selected firms.

## 9. Looking at credit constraint gives a similar court result

**Exhibit**

| Outcome | Difference per 10 more confidence points | 95% interval |
|---|---:|---:|
| Firms holding credit | −0.67 pp | [−2.41, 1.07] |
| Firms classified as fully credit constrained | −0.01 pp | [−1.27, 1.25] |

**Speaker notes**

Credit holding reflects who wants and obtains a loan. The credit-constraint measure also uses financing and application information. Both confidence intervals include zero. A bounded credit-share model and one-country-at-a-time influence checks in the appendix give the same broad reading.

## 10. Ask about recovery and lending decisions directly

**Exhibit**

| Audience | What to measure |
|---|---|
| Banks | Time and amount recovered after default |
| Governments | Application outcomes alongside specific public transactions |
| Business associations | Reasons firms forgo applying and where they encounter barriers |

**Speaker notes**

These measures would help distinguish problems in courts from changes in borrower demand, bank decisions and public administration. The survey results can guide the questions, while measuring recovery and applications gives managers evidence they can act on.

## 11. Court confidence by itself tells us little about who has credit

**Exhibit**

> Country comparisons give examples in both directions. The estimated average link remains uncertain.

**Speaker notes**

The survey provides a comparison of credit already held across economies. The court-obstacle comparison over time is also inconclusive. Bribery is more strongly associated with credit in the basic model and much less so after additional controls. Tracking actual credit and enforcement decisions would help answer the business question.
