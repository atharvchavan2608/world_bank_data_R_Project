# Trust in Courts, Trust in Credit?

## 1. Credit holding varies widely across economies

**Exhibit**

A single horizontal range with three labels. Minimum **2.1%**, economy mean **36.5%**, maximum **88.5%** of firms with a loan or line of credit.

**Speaker notes**

The range comes from the recent-wave sample and the unit is an economy. Ask whether court perceptions, bribery exposure and the origin of commercial law help explain the variation. The published bank-credit indicator comes from a question about credit from a financial institution. Firms may lack credit because they chose to forgo borrowing, could not qualify, or faced other barriers.

## 2. The main analysis compares indicators from the same survey wave

**Exhibit**

| Coverage | Economies |
|---|---:|
| Latest archived credit observation | 181 |
| Recent wave with all nine pitch survey measures | 163 |
| Recent wave with usable legal-origin classification | 129 |

**Speaker notes**

Each row of the analysis uses published national totals from one economy and one survey wave. The regression gives each economy equal weight. Some economies have only older waves. The fall from 163 to 129 is due to missing legal-origin classifications, concentrated in Europe and Central Asia.

## 3. Court doubt alone explains little of the variation in credit holding

**Exhibit**

| Model | Credit-share difference per 10 points more court doubt | 95% interval |
|---|---:|---:|
| Court doubt alone | −0.60 pp | [−2.59, +1.39] |
| With bribery and firm size | +0.67 pp | [−1.07, +2.41] |

**Speaker notes**

The World Bank scale rises with disagreement that courts are independent and impartial. Both models use the same economies. The simple model explains about 0.25% of the variation. The change in sign and the intervals give little support for the expected negative slope.

## 4. Bribery has the larger association in the core model

**Exhibit**

One horizontal dot-and-interval chart centered on zero. Plot credit-share differences for a one-standard-deviation increase in each measure.

| Measure | Credit-share difference | 95% interval |
|---|---:|---:|
| Court doubt | +1.10 pp | [−1.76, +3.96] |
| Bribery exposure | −7.65 pp | [−10.17, −5.13] |

**Speaker notes**

Both measures move in the adverse direction and use their standard deviations on the same sample. The signed difference is 8.75 points with a 95% interval of 4.89 to 12.62. This comparison concerns cross-economy associations. It supplies no ranking of reforms.

## 5. Adjusting for geography and digital adoption shrinks the bribery estimate

**Exhibit**

| Terms added to the core model | Credit-share difference per 10 points more bribery | 95% interval |
|---|---:|---:|
| Core model | −7.06 pp | [−9.38, −4.73] |
| Region and survey cohort | −2.81 pp | [−5.53, −0.08] |
| Firms with their own website | −2.91 pp | [−5.76, −0.06] |
| Region, cohort and websites | −1.43 pp | [−4.14, +1.28] |

**Speaker notes**

The economies are held fixed. The court-versus-bribery contrast becomes imprecise when region and cohort are added, or when website adoption is added. The bribery interval itself spans zero with both adjustments. Website ownership captures a dimension of business development and could itself respond to institutions or access to credit.

## 6. The common-law comparison remains imprecise

**Exhibit**

| Comparison group for English common-law origin | Economies | Credit-share difference | 95% interval |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| All documented other origins | 129 | −4.45 pp | [−11.34, +2.44] |
| Named civil-code families | 119 | −4.69 pp | [−11.80, +2.43] |

**Speaker notes**

Compare economies at the same observed court doubt, bribery and firm size. The legal classification refers to company law or commercial codes. The wider reference group includes Socialist origin. Both intervals span zero, and a historical category can capture persistent differences in economic conditions and colonial history.

## 7. Durbin–Watson changes when the economies are reordered

**Exhibit**

| Core residual order | Durbin–Watson |
|---|---:|
| Economy code | 1.916 |
| Survey wave, then code | 1.643 |
| Region, then code | 1.711 |

**Speaker notes**

Neighboring rows have no natural time-series meaning. Survey-cohort controls and region comparisons address more plausible sources of similarity across economies. The core variance test gives p = .862 and its maximum VIF is 1.07. Conventional and HC3 inference give the same qualitative core results. There are seven uneven regional groups, which limits cluster-based inference.

## 8. A bounded model gives a similar core association

**Exhibit**

| Credit-share model | Difference per 10 points more bribery | 95% interval |
|---|---:|---:|
| OLS | −7.06 pp | [−9.38, −4.73] |
| Fractional logit | −7.47 pp | [−9.89, −5.05] |

**Speaker notes**

OLS gives one negative fitted share. Fractional logit keeps fitted shares between zero and 100 percent. Omitting the most influential economies individually also preserves the core pattern. These checks address model form and influence. The attenuation under additional controls remains the main challenge to the core estimate.

## 9. Higher bribery also coincides with more fully constrained firms

**Exhibit**

One estimate and interval. **+4.57 pp** fully credit constrained per 10 points more bribery, 95% interval **[+2.14, +7.01]**.

**Speaker notes**

The constraint measure combines financing sources and evidence from application and nonapplication questions. It provides a related view of unmet credit need. The observational comparison cannot distinguish firms discouraged from applying from changes in lender decisions or financing terms.

## 10. The survey points to different questions for governments, banks and firms

**Exhibit**

| Audience | What to measure directly |
|---|---|
| Governments | Bribe requests tied to specific interactions and credit application outcomes |
| Banks | Recovery time, collectability of collateral and borrower information |
| Firms and business associations | Application costs, collateral requests and dispute duration |

**Speaker notes**

These measures would help identify the channels behind the aggregate associations. The results offer limited evidence about which part of judicial performance matters for lenders. Better application and recovery data could support a policy or underwriting decision that the current cross-section cannot identify.

## 11. The cross-section supports a narrower conclusion than the core fit

**Exhibit**

| Question | Evidence across the study |
|---|---|
| Are court perceptions associated with credit holding? | Estimates are imprecise across specifications. |
| Is bribery the stronger correlate? | Yes in the core comparison. The difference weakens under added controls. |
| Does English common-law origin matter? | The estimated differences have wide intervals. |

**Speaker notes**

National income is unmeasured, and credit conditions can influence both business perceptions and firm behavior. The analysis covers eligible formal firms. To evaluate a reform, track lending and enforcement outcomes around credible changes in institutions.
