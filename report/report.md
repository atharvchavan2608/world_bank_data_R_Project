# Trust in Courts, Trust in Credit?

*Court perceptions, bribery exposure and business credit in the World Bank Enterprise Surveys*

## 1. Introduction and Literature Review

A lender evaluating a business loan needs to know whether it can recover value if a borrower defaults. Courts enter that calculation through expected recovery time, the predictability of a ruling and the ability to enforce it. Managers can observe parts of that system through commercial disputes and through their peers. Their assessment of judicial independence may help explain why business credit reaches a larger share of firms in some economies. Bribe requests by public officials capture a different part of the operating environment. They can consume working capital, create uncertainty about permits or taxes and make it harder for firms to document their operations.

The law-and-finance literature provides a basis for these mechanisms. La Porta et al. (1998) document differences in investor protections and enforcement across legal families. Their work motivates examining the origins of company law alongside contemporary business perceptions. Djankov et al. (2007) find that creditor rights and institutions for sharing borrower information are associated with larger private credit markets. Their outcome is credit relative to GDP. An economy can have a deep credit market and still lend to a narrow segment of firms, so we examine the share of firms that hold credit. Visaria (2009) uses the staggered introduction of debt recovery tribunals in India to find that faster enforcement reduced delinquency and lowered interest rates on larger loans. That evidence supports an enforcement channel in a specific setting. The cross-economy survey comparison here has a different source of variation and a different outcome.

Beck et al. (2005) show that smaller firms are particularly exposed to financial, legal and corruption obstacles to growth. This motivates accounting for firm size and distinguishing institutional indicators. The bribery measure here concerns firms' interactions with public authorities. It captures a distinct exposure from the corruption in bank lending studied by Barth et al. (2009). Legal origin presents a separate question. La Porta et al. (2008) review how inherited legal traditions might influence policy and economic outcomes. Klerman et al. (2011) argue that colonial history and legal origin can operate through overlapping channels. The origin dummy is therefore a historical classification whose coefficient may reflect other persistent differences.

We ask whether more favorable perceptions of court impartiality are associated with greater credit holding, whether bribery has a larger association on a comparable scale, and whether credit holding differs by English common-law origin. The core estimates show a negative bribery association while estimates for courts and legal origin are imprecise. The bribery association weakens considerably when we account for differences in region, survey cohort and firm digital adoption.

## 2. Methodology and Model Specification

The unit of analysis is economy $i$ at one observed Enterprise Survey wave. Write the simple population regression function as $E[Y_i\mid D_i]=\alpha+\beta D_i$ if the conditional mean is linear, where $Y_i$ is the percentage of firms with a loan or line of credit and $D_i$ is court doubt, the published index of disagreement with the impartiality and independence of courts. The stochastic outcome equals this mean plus an error. We first estimate the simple model by ordinary least squares (OLS). The multivariate population working model is

$$
Y_i=\alpha+\beta D_i+\gamma B_i+\theta\log(W_i)+\delta L_i+u_i,
$$

where $B_i$ is the percentage of firms experiencing a bribe request in the covered transactions, $W_i$ is average permanent full-time-equivalent employment per firm, and $L_i$ indicates English common-law origin. The disturbance $u_i$ includes unobserved credit demand, national income, banking practices, the mix of firms and sampling and measurement error. It allows otherwise similar observed economies to have different credit shares.

OLS minimizes the sum of squared residuals. It weights each economy equally. The firm sampling weights used to compute each national indicator have a different purpose. At the economy level, a classical model assumes linearity in parameters, a full-rank design, conditional mean-zero errors, constant conditional error variance and zero cross-economy error covariance. Exact classical finite-sample tests additionally use normal errors conditional on the regressors. Independent sampling is a working approximation, since economies may share shocks. If the conditional mean is nonlinear, OLS still estimates a best linear projection under appropriate sampling and moment conditions. Orthogonality to its included regressors defines that projection. It provides no guarantee that the slope represents a causal response to a change in institutions.

The core multivariate fit holds bribery and log employment fixed when evaluating court doubt. Employment enters in logs to reduce the leverage of unusually large averages and permit a proportional comparison. A separate fit includes the common-law dummy. We first refit the core equation on the exact legal-origin sample to distinguish a change in coverage from the addition of a regressor. The dummy coefficient compares fitted credit shares across origin groups at equal observed courts, bribery and employment. We show both a pooled comparison with all scored origins and a comparison restricted to English and named civil-code families. The core uses three slopes and 159 residual degrees of freedom. The full model uses nine slopes and 119 residual degrees of freedom on the QoG sample. Extra terms and sparse group overlap can still widen intervals.

The full pitch specification also conditions on severe court obstacles, court or alternative dispute resolution use, dispute incidence, collateral and reported lack of need for credit. Court obstacles offer a related measure of perceptions. Dispute-resolution use is observed among firms with disputes. Collateral is observed on loans that exist, and reported lack of need combines demand and firms' available funds. These controls can reflect outcomes of the credit and institutional environment, so adding them can change the comparison or introduce selection. We show the extended model and its improved fit as a specification check. We use the smaller core model as the main descriptive comparison.

For the court-doubt, bribery and origin slopes, the individual null is equality to zero, and the alternative is a nonzero slope. The expected direction is negative for court doubt and bribery, and positive for common law under the legal-origin hypothesis. Joint F tests concern either all slopes or the court-doubt and bribery slopes, plus the origin slope where included. We report classical tests and HC3 inference, which adjusts covariance for residual variance and leverage (MacKinnon & White, 1985). The HC3 t and Wald F tests use residual degrees of freedom as an approximation under independent economy errors. Reported confidence intervals are 95%. We compare adverse one-standard-deviation changes on the same sample and use the joint covariance matrix to test equality of the signed effects. A separate exploratory test evaluates absolute magnitudes when bribery is negatively associated with credit. We also report partial $R^2$ for the two indicators.

## 3. Data Description

The World Bank Enterprise Surveys cover eligible formal, nonagricultural private-sector establishments. The survey typically covers registered firms with at least five employees in selected sectors, using stratified sampling by location, industry and size. It excludes much informal and microenterprise activity. The World Bank uses survey weights to produce aggregate indicators. The regression uses those published aggregates, with one observation per economy. The question behind FIN14 refers to a loan or line from a financial institution. Its published bank-credit label is convenient shorthand. Current holdings combine prior demand, application, approval and the duration of credit relationships. [World Bank survey methodology](https://www.enterprisesurveys.org/en/methodology)

The Data360 files provide national and subgroup observations in long format. We require the national-total code `_T` for sex, age, urbanisation and all three combined breakdown dimensions. We verify the annual, normal-status, unscaled WB_ES observations and unique indicator–economy–year keys. We anchor each economy to the most recent national observation of FIN14 and join each predictor to that economy and survey year. The latest wave in the union of archived indicator files agrees with the FIN14 anchor for all 181 economies. We use the archive field `TIME_PERIOD` as the survey-year key. We cannot recover separate survey identifiers from the CSVs. [World Bank Data360 Enterprise Surveys](https://data360.worldbank.org/en/dataset/WB_ES)

The primary analysis covers the 163 economies with latest waves in 2021–2026. All nine pitch survey indicators are observed at the same wave for each economy. The credit share averages 36.5%, with a standard deviation of 19.6 points and a range of 2.1% to 88.5%. The 18 economies with earlier latest waves averaged 30.9%. Seven of those economies are in Latin America and the Caribbean. The recent window therefore trades some representativeness for temporal and questionnaire comparability. Even within it, differences in survey timing and questionnaire wording can matter. Starting in 2023 leaves 158 economies, and starting in 2024 leaves 113. The pitch's survey-year counts match exactly. Of its displayed descriptive statistics, the BREADY_DISP3 mean and CRIME9 standard deviation differ at the last digit under direct rounding. Both match with two-stage rounding. The original computation is unavailable.

The World Bank's indicator guide confirms that the current BREADY_DISP3 scale assigns zero to agreement and 100 to disagreement with the statement that courts are independent and impartial in resolving commercial disputes. The guide records a September 2025 scale revision. The pitch's favorable label would reverse the interpretation. The archived values reach their minimum in Kuwait and maximum in North Macedonia. On the legal-origin sample, doubt and the severe-obstacle share correlate at 0.52. These measures capture related perceptions, with CRIME9 asking how severe a constraint courts are to operations. The DISP6 check scores responses across the severity scale. GRAFT3 covers six types of public transactions and codes refusals as affirmative. Reporting behavior and transaction exposure may differ by economy. [World Bank indicator guide](https://www.enterprisesurveys.org/content/dam/enterprisesurveys/documents/methodology/Indicator-Description.pdf)

The January 2022 Quality of Government (QoG) archive (Teorell et al., 2022) attributes legal origin to La Porta et al. (1999). It classifies the origin of company law or the commercial code. English origin accounts for 46 of the 129 usable codes. The 34 economies without usable codes include 31 matched records with a blank code and three without a matched record. More than half of the unclassified economies are in Europe and Central Asia. The 129 classified economies and the unclassified economies have nearly identical average credit holding, although their geographic composition differs. Removing the 10 Socialist-origin observations from the comparator leaves 119 economies for the English versus named civil-code comparison. The pitch assigned one to eight unclassified economies and zero to the others. We test that fallback and an extension with documented descriptors, including Germany's commercial-code origin. Mixed systems such as Namibia and Vanuatu remain unclassified in that extension. [QoG legal-origin codebook](https://datafinder.qog.gu.se/variable/lp_legor), [classification audit](../config/README.md)

## 4. Hypotheses Testing and Results

The simple regression associates a ten-point increase in court doubt with 0.60 percentage points lower credit holding. Its HC3 interval is [−2.59, 1.39]. The single-restriction HC3 test gives $F(1,161)=0.355$ and $p=.552$, equivalent to the squared slope t statistic. The simple equation explains about 0.25% of the observed between-economy variation in credit, and adjusted $R^2$ is slightly negative. In the core model, $R^2$ rises to 0.215 and adjusted $R^2$ to 0.200 on the same sample. Holding bribery and employment fixed, a ten-point increase in court doubt is associated with 0.67 points higher credit holding [−1.07, 2.41], $t(159)=0.76$, $p=.449$. The shift in sign and the width of the interval give little basis for the expected negative association.

A ten-point increase in bribery incidence is associated with 7.06 points lower credit holding [−9.38, −4.73], $t(159)=-6.00$, $p<.001$. Doubling mean employment is associated with 6.03 points higher credit holding [2.90, 9.15] at the same institutional scores. This is an economy-level firm-size comparison. The classical overall test gives $F(3,159)=14.53$ and the HC3 test gives $F(3,159)=18.42$, both with $p<.001$. The HC3 institutional-block test gives $F(2,159)=18.17$, $p<.001$. These results indicate joint explanatory power. Evidence about which institutional measure contributes comes from the individual and contrast tests.

On the same core sample, one sample standard deviation more court doubt corresponds to 1.10 points higher credit holding [−1.76, 3.96], and one standard deviation more bribery to 7.65 points lower [−10.17, −5.13]. The signed court-minus-bribery difference is 8.75 points [4.89, 12.62], $p<.001$. The exploratory test that the negatively signed bribery association exceeds court doubt in absolute value gives $p<.001$ in this specification. Bribery accounts for 15.3% of the residual variation from the model omitting bribery. The analogous partial $R^2$ for court doubt is 0.38%. These measures describe incremental in-sample fit and depend on the specific indicators and controls.

On the fixed QoG sample, adding origin increases $R^2$ from 0.217 to 0.227 and adjusted $R^2$ from 0.198 to 0.202. The conditional common-law difference is −4.45 points [−11.34, 2.44], $p=.204$, against all documented other origins. Against the civil-code families it is −4.69 [−11.80, 2.43], $p=.195$. The intervals accommodate economically relevant differences, particularly in the negative direction. With cohort and region controls, the pooled comparison moves closer to zero and becomes less precise. The audited and pitch classification sensitivities also span zero. The full set of pitch variables raises adjusted $R^2$ to 0.340 on the QoG sample. The ten-point bribery association moderates to −4.62 [−7.64, −1.60]. The gain in fit coexists with concerns about jointly determined controls.

### Diagnostics and remedies

The studentized Breusch–Pagan test uses squared residuals to assess whether conditional variance varies with the regressors (Koenker, 1981). The core statistic is $\chi^2(3)=0.75$, $p=.862$. For the full terms on the QoG sample it is $\chi^2(9)=16.82$, $p=.052$. There is little evidence of the specified variance relationship in the core. The full model is near the conventional threshold. The national indicators can have different sampling precision, and the archive lacks comparable indicator variances for feasible precision weighting. HC3 changes the core bribery slope standard error from 0.131 to 0.118, retaining the same slope. It preserves the substantive conclusions of the classical individual tests. Unequal error variance alone affects inference and efficiency under exogeneity. It does not supply evidence about omitted-variable bias.

Durbin–Watson assesses residual differences along an ordering (Durbin & Watson, 1950). Ordered by economy code, the core statistic is 1.916. It is 1.643 for survey year then code, and 1.711 for region then code. Conditional reference simulations give nominal two-sided p-values of .586, .025 and .065, respectively. Each uses the fitted design and independent Gaussian errors of constant variance. These comparisons demonstrate order dependence in a cross-section with no natural series. The smaller year-sorted result motivates examining survey vintage. Cross-economy dependence is more plausibly regional, for example when bank funding conditions or policy shocks move together. Region controls and leave-one-region-out comparisons probe this concern. The region-clustered standard error for the ten-point bribery comparison is 1.72 points, compared with 1.18 for HC3. Seven uneven regions, with groups as small as two, make conventional clustered tests unreliable.

The largest VIF is 1.07 in the core and 1.74 with the full pitch terms. Variance inflation is modest, so court measure selection rests primarily on interpretation. Raw employment is right-skewed, with a mean of 39.0 workers, median 33.1 and maximum 318.5. Its skewness falls from 5.35 to 0.64 with logs. The level-employment sensitivity leaves the core institutional conclusions similar. The HC3 fitted-powers check gives $p=.226$ for the core. One core OLS prediction is −2.34%, for Kiribati. A fractional logit enforces the range of shares (Papke & Wooldridge, 1996). Its average ten-point bribery association is −7.47 [−9.89, −5.05], and the court-doubt association is 0.84 [−0.87, 2.56]. Omitting Israel, Kiribati or Guatemala individually preserves the main sign and interval patterns. These economies illustrate different sources of influence through residual size or leverage.

## 5. Findings and Interpretation

Bribery is the stronger correlate of credit holding in the core comparison. When omitting any single region, shortening the sample window or replacing court doubt with the severe-obstacle share, the bribery interval excludes zero and the court interval spans it. The related FIN23 outcome, full credit constraint, also rises with bribery. A ten-point difference in bribery is associated with 4.57 points more fully constrained firms [2.14, 7.01]. FIN23 incorporates financing sources, applications and reasons for nonapplication. It supplies a complementary view of unmet credit need. The same-wave cross-section cannot distinguish whether exposure discourages applications or co-occurs with stricter lending.

The magnitude depends on whether differences between regions and levels of business development are included. Region and cohort controls reduce the ten-point bribery estimate to −2.81 [−5.53, −0.08]. Adding the share of firms with their own website to the core alone gives −2.91 [−5.76, −0.06]. With both sets of controls, the estimate is −1.43 [−4.14, 1.28]. The standardized court-minus-bribery interval already spans zero under each of these adjustments. Website adoption may index firms' access to markets, infrastructure or managerial capacity. It may also respond to institutions or financing. Accordingly the combined fit challenges the magnitude of the core comparison and has its own conditioning limitations.

National income, financial depth and state capacity can affect both institutional reports and credit holding. The eligible analytical inputs leave national income unmeasured. If higher income coincides with more credit holding and less bribery after the included controls, its omission could exaggerate the negative bribery association. An omitted-variable benchmark gives this concern a scale (Cinelli & Hazlett, 2020). For one omitted variable to move the core bribery slope to zero with equally strong residual associations, it would need to explain about 34.5% of the residual variation in each of the bribery and outcome equations, conditional on their relevant controls and with the adverse sign. The analogous court threshold is about 6.0%. This benchmark does not measure the strength of national income's relationship with the two variables. Credit may also improve firms' capacity to formalize or insulate them from informal payments. Aggregate perceptions and credit shares can reflect the experience of different respondent groups. Those limitations prevent firm-level or causal interpretation.

For governments, the results identify places to look for barriers. Loan application and rejection data linked to specific licensing or tax interactions would help distinguish credit demand from supply and test whether those interactions raise financing costs. For banks, the court and legal-origin results suggest a rationale for measuring local recovery time, collateral enforceability and borrower information directly before incorporating enforcement into underwriting. For firms and business associations, evidence on application costs, collateral requests and dispute duration would clarify which difficulties are shared and which vary by lender or location. Wider coverage of informal firms would be needed to extend these conclusions beyond the survey population.

## 6. Conclusion

In the latest-wave Enterprise Survey cross-section, court doubt has an imprecise association with credit holding across the models considered. The English common-law comparisons are also imprecise under both broad and civil-code reference groups. Bribery has a negative, economically substantial association in the core model and a positive association with full credit constraint. Conditioning jointly on geography, survey cohort and website adoption sharply reduces the credit-holding association and the distinction from court doubt. The results establish a pattern in the survey aggregates and a limit on what that pattern can support. Identifying the role of specific reforms would require direct measures of enforcement and financial decisions together with credible variation over time.
